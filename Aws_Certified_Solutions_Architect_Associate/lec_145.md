## 1️⃣ What Is Server Access Logging? 📜

> 💡 **Core Idea:** When enabled, S3 **writes a log record every time someone makes a request** to your bucket. This lets you see **who is using your objects**.

> 🧒 **In simple words:** It's the **visitor register at a building's front desk**. Every visitor signs in, so later you can check **who came, when, and what they asked for**. 📒

> 📝 *Bonus detail (not in the lecture): each log record typically includes the **requester**, the **bucket name**, the **time of the request**, the **action** performed, and the **response status**.*

---

## 2️⃣ Enabling It ⚙️

Navigate: **Bucket → Properties → Server access logging → Enable**

| Setting | Value in the Demo |
|---|---|
| 🎯 **Target bucket** (where the logs are stored) | The **same bucket**: `my-bucket-in28minutes` |
| 🏷️ **Prefix** | `logs/` |

### 🏷️ What's the Prefix For?

> 💡 The prefix is **added to the start of every log file's key**. With `logs/`, all log files appear together under a "logs" folder, so they don't get mixed up with your normal objects.

```
my-bucket-in28minutes/
   ├── 2030/course0.png           ← your own objects
   └── logs/2025-01-01-...         ← log files (thanks to the prefix)
```

> 🔗 **Recall Lecture 143:** Remember, there are **no real folders**. `logs/` is just the **start of the key**, so the console shows it as a folder.

Click **Save**.

---

## 3️⃣ Giving S3 Permission to Write the Logs 🔐

> ⚠️ **Enabling logging isn't enough.** The service that writes the logs also needs **permission to put files into your bucket**.

### 📝 The Steps (as shown in the demo)

```
1️⃣ Bucket → Permissions → Access Control List (ACL)
2️⃣ Find the "S3 Log Delivery" group
3️⃣ Give it permissions (the demo granted all, including read and write)
4️⃣ Save
```

### 🧒 In Simple Words

> 📬 The **S3 Log Delivery group** is like a **mail carrier** whose job is to drop the visitor register pages into your bucket. If you don't give the carrier **permission to enter the building**, the pages never get delivered. So you hand the carrier a **pass**.

> 🆕 **Modern note:** AWS has changed this since the lecture was recorded. **New buckets have ACLs turned off by default**, and AWS now recommends granting log delivery permission with a **bucket policy** instead (and the console often sets this up for you). The idea is identical (*"let the log delivery service write here"*), but the clicks differ. If you don't see the ACL option, that's why. Check the current AWS docs for the exact steps.

## ❓ Extra Important Question: "The demo stores logs in the SAME bucket being logged. Is that a good idea?"

> 💡 **Answer:** It works for a demo, but **best practice is to use a separate bucket just for logs**. Here's why:
>
> - 🔁 **Logs about logs:** Writing a log file into the same bucket is itself a request to that bucket, which can create **more log entries about the log files**. Over time, your logs can **grow quickly** and make it **harder to spot real activity**.
> - 🧹 **Easier management:** A dedicated log bucket can have its **own lifecycle rules** (such as deleting logs after 90 days), its **own permissions**, and its own **retention settings**, without touching your real data.
> - 🔐 **Security:** Logs often contain sensitive information, like **who accessed what**. Keeping them in a separate, tightly controlled bucket reduces the risk.
>
> 🧒 **In simple words:** You wouldn't keep the **visitor register inside the same locker** the visitors are asking about. You'd keep it at the front desk, in its own drawer. 🗄️
