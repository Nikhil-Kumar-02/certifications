## 4️⃣ The Self-Healing Experiment 🔥

> 🧪 **The Test:** Let's manually kill one of our two running tasks and see what happens.

```
1️⃣ Go to Tasks → pick one → click Stop
2️⃣ Task's "Last Status" becomes RUNNING, but "Desired Status" becomes STOPPED
3️⃣ A moment later, it moves to DEPROVISIONING
4️⃣ Meanwhile, a NEW task is launched to replace it
```

### 📰 What the Events Log Shows

```
🔴 Service starts draining connections on the killed task
🔍 Service detects: only 1 active task, but 2 are DESIRED
🟢 Service launches a new task to compensate
✅ New task becomes ready → steady state reached again
```

> 🎯 **Key Insight:** This is **self-healing** in action! The service's entire job is to ensure the **desired count** of tasks is *always* met — kill one, and it's automatically replaced.

## ❓ Extra Important Question: "When my killed task got replaced, did users experience any downtime?"

> 💡 **Answer:** In a properly configured setup with a load balancer (as in this demo), the answer is generally **no** — or at most a very brief blip. Here's why: the load balancer only routes traffic to **healthy** targets. The moment the killed task starts "draining connections," the load balancer stops sending new traffic to it (while letting existing in-flight requests finish), and continues routing all new traffic to the remaining healthy task. Once the replacement task passes its health checks, the load balancer adds it back into rotation. The brief window where you're running on just 1 of 2 desired tasks means *reduced capacity*, not necessarily *downtime* — though under heavy load, that temporary capacity reduction could cause slower response times until the replacement task is ready.
