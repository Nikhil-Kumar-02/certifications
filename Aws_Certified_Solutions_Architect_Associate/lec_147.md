## 1️⃣ Recap: The Steps to Host a Static Website 🌐

| # | Step | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1️⃣ | **Upload** the website content | The files to serve |
| 2️⃣ | **Enable static website hosting** on the bucket | Turns the bucket into a web host |
| 3️⃣ | **Disable "Block public access"** (it's on by default) | Removes the master lock so public access is *possible* |
| 4️⃣ | Add a **bucket policy** allowing public read | Actually *grants* the access |

> 📝 *The lecture lists the steps in this order; in the previous lecture we enabled hosting before uploading. The order of the first two doesn't matter. What matters is that **all four are done**.*

> 🎯 **Key Insight:** S3 has **many checks and balances** to keep your content secure. Making something public is **never an accident**. You have to take **several deliberate steps**.

---

## 2️⃣ Reading the Bucket Policy 📜

The important parts of the policy we configured:

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 👥 **Who** → everybody (`*`) | **Anyone**, including anonymous visitors |
| 🔧 **What** → `GetObject` | The S3 API call that **reads (fetches) one object** |
| 🪣 **Where** → everything inside our bucket | Every object in that specific bucket (the example used `mybucket`; yours has your own bucket's name) |

### 🗣️ In One Sentence

> *"Allow **everybody** to **GetObject** on **everything inside this bucket**."*

> 🧒 **In simple words:** It's a notice stuck on the warehouse room's door: **"Anyone may take a look at what's inside, but nobody may change anything."** 📋

---

## 3️⃣ Resource-Based Policies 🏷️

> 💡 **Core Idea:** A **bucket policy** is called a **resource-based policy** because it is **attached to a resource**, and here the resource is the **S3 bucket**.

```
📜 The policy lives ON the bucket
   (the bucket is the "resource")
```

### 🧒 In Simple Words: Rules Posted on the Door vs Rules in a Person's File 🚪📁

| Type | Where the Rules Live | 🧒 Picture |
|---|---|---|
| 🏷️ **Resource-based policy** (like a bucket policy) | **Attached to the resource** | A **sign on the door** of the room: "These people may enter" |
| 👤 **Identity-based policy** (IAM policies, coming later) | **Attached to a user, group, or role** | A **permission slip in a person's file**: "This person may enter these rooms" |

> 📌 **Exam Tip:** If a policy is **attached to the S3 bucket itself**, it's a **bucket policy**, which is a **resource-based policy**.

---

## 4️⃣ When Do You Use Bucket Policies? 🎯

> 💡 Bucket policies **control access to your buckets and objects**. The two classic scenarios:

| Scenario | Example |
|---|---|
| 🌍 **Make a bucket (or its content) public** | Our **static website** |
| 🤝 **Cross-account access** | Give **another AWS account** access to your bucket |

### 🧒 In Simple Words: Cross-Account Access

> 🏢 Imagine you own **Building A** (your AWS account) and a partner company owns **Building B** (another AWS account). You want their staff to collect files from **your storeroom**. You can't change the rules inside *their* building, so you put a note **on your storeroom's door**: *"Employees from Building B may take files from here."* That note is a **bucket policy** granting cross-account access.

---

## ❓ Extra Important Question: "If I have an IAM policy for a user AND a bucket policy on the bucket, which one wins?"

> 💡 **Answer:** Good question. We'll cover IAM in depth later in the course, but here's the basic idea:
>
> - 🔴 **An explicit "Deny" always wins**, from any policy.
> - 🟢 **Within the same AWS account:** access is allowed if **either** the user's IAM policy **or** the bucket policy allows it (and nothing denies it).
> - 🟡 **Across accounts:** **both sides** must allow it: the bucket policy in the owner's account (letting the other account in) **and** a permission in the other account for the user.
>
> 🧒 **In simple words:** Within one building, either the **door sign** or the **person's permission slip** can let you in. Between two buildings, you need **the sign on the door AND your own company's slip** saying you're allowed to go.
